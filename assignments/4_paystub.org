* Pay Stub Calculator

This assignment uses only chapter 2 material: variables and assignment,
arithmetic, ~type()~, ~round()~, ~str()~, ~len()~, string repetition
with ~*~, comparison operators, and ~print~. No ~if~, no loops, no
function definitions.

The whole program is driven by two values:

#+begin_example
  hourly_rate  = 15.0    # dollars per hour
  hours_worked = 40      # hours this week
#+end_example

Each step in parts 1-4 below builds on the variables from the step
before - that is the point of the exercise: a program is a chain of
statements, each one using the results of the last. In part 5, you are
then asked to put together a Python script.

Open a new Jupyter notebook and title it =Pay_Stub_Calculator.ipynb=.

** Part 1 - Gross pay

- Assign =hourly_rate= and =hours_worked= as above.
- Compute =gross_pay = hourly_rate * hours_worked=.
- Print =gross_pay= and its ~type()~ on two labelled lines, e.g.
  =Gross pay = 600.0= then =Type = <class 'float'>.=

You should see =600.0=, not =600=. Make sure =hourly_rate= is written
with a decimal point (=15.0=, not =15=) so the result is a ~float~.

** Part 2 - Tax and net pay

- Assign =tax_rate = 0.20= (20% written as a decimal - *not* =20=).
- Compute =tax_amount = gross_pay * tax_rate=.
- Compute =net_pay = gross_pay - tax_amount=.
- Wrap =tax_amount= and =net_pay= in ~round()~ with =2= decimal places
  (the tax rate is not money - leave it as =0.20=). These numbers are already clean, so
  nothing visible changes - but rounding money to the nearest cent is
  the habit to build.

** Part 3 - A readable pay stub

Print three labelled lines so the ~$~ signs line up:

#+begin_example
Gross pay: $600.0
Tax (20%): $120.0
Net pay:   $480.0
#+end_example

Build each line with ~+~ and ~str()~ - the numbers must come from your
variables, not be typed inside the quotes.

Then add a *title* and a *separator* rule above and below it. Define

#+begin_example
  title = "Weekly Pay Stub"
#+end_example

and make the separator a row of dashes *exactly as long as the title*,
using ="-" * len(title)=. The finished output:

#+begin_example
---------------
Weekly Pay Stub
---------------
Gross pay: $600.0
Tax (20%): $120.0
Net pay:   $480.0
---------------
#+end_example

** Part 4 - Sanity checks

Comparison operators return a ~bool~ (~True~ or ~False~). Print the
result of each:

- Is =net_pay= greater than zero?
- Is =net_pay= greater than =gross_pay=?
- Does =tax_amount= equal =gross_pay * tax_rate=?

The output should look like this:
#+begin_example
Is net_pay greater than zero? True
Is net_pay greater than gross_pay? False
Does tax_amount equal gross_pay * tax_rate? True
#+end_example
  
** Part 5 - Overtime

Parts 1-4 assume the worker did exactly 40 hours. Now suppose they
worked *more*. Any hours beyond a standard 40-hour week are paid at
*1.5 times* the hourly rate.

Extend your program for a worker who did *46* hours:

- the first 40 hours are paid at the normal rate;
- the extra hours are paid at 1.5x the rate;
- gross pay is the sum of the two;
- tax and net pay follow exactly as before;
- the stub gains a =Regular:= line and an =Overtime:= line above
  =Gross pay:=.

The "no bare numbers" rule still holds: the =40= and the =1.5= need
their own named variables. Assume the worker always does at least 40
hours - you have not covered ~if~ yet.

Expected output (for =hourly_rate = 15.0=, =hours_worked = 46=):

#+begin_example
---------------
Weekly Pay Stub
---------------
Regular:   $600.0
Overtime:  $135.0
Gross pay: $735.0
Tax (20%): $147.0
Net pay:   $588.0
---------------
#+end_example
