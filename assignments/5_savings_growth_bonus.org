* Savings Growth Calculator

A bank offers two ways of growing a deposit over time: *simple
interest*, where you earn the same amount every year on your original
deposit, and *compound interest*, where each year's interest is added
to the balance before the next year's interest is calculated - so you
earn interest on your interest. Over a long enough time, the
difference is dramatic.

You're building a small toolkit of functions that reports both, for a
given deposit, rate, and number of years. Unlike the certificate
printer, this one doesn't tell you the internal steps - you'll need to
work out the arithmetic (and the right parameters) yourself. Look up
"simple interest formula" and "compound interest formula" if you don't
already know them; this is exactly the kind of thing programmers look
up rather than memorize.

Open a new Jupyter notebook and title it =Savings_Growth.ipynb=.

** What to build

Three functions, each with a docstring that states the formula it
uses in words:

- A function that computes and prints the value of a deposit after
  some number of years, growing at *simple* interest.
- A function that computes and prints the value of the same deposit,
  growing at *compound* interest, compounded once per year. Give its
  "number of years" parameter a sensible default, so it can be called
  either way.
- A function that takes a deposit, a rate, and a number of years, and
  calls both of the above to print a side-by-side comparison -
  showing the starting numbers first, then both results.

Money should always print rounded to two decimal places, with a
dollar sign; the rate should print as a percentage, not a decimal
fraction.

For a deposit of =$1000=, a rate of =5%=, and =10= years, calling your
comparison function should produce exactly:

#+begin_example
Starting balance:  $1000.00 at 5.0% for 10 years
Simple interest:   $1500.00 after 10 years
Compound interest: $1628.89 after 10 years
#+end_example

Then call your compound-interest function on its own, on the same
deposit and rate, *without* specifying the number of years, and
confirm it falls back to the same =10=-year result.

** Bonus-within-the-bonus (optional)

Call your comparison function a second time for the same deposit and
years, but at a much higher rate (say =8%=, a risky stock fund, versus
the =5%= CD above). By how many dollars does the compound-interest gap
between the two rates differ after =10= years? Answer in a comment.
