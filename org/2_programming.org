** Learning how to solve problems

For example:
#+begin_quote
When a party of four splits a bill of $45.50 with a tip of 15% evenly,
how much does every one have to pay?
#+end_quote
#+begin_src python :results output :exports both :session *Python* :python python3
  bill  = 45.50
  tax   = 15
  party = 4
  total = bill + (bill * tax/100)
  share = total/party
  print(share)
  print(f"The per-person share is: ${share:.2f}")
#+end_src

** Learning how to generalize solutions

For example:
#+begin_quote
When a party splits its bill with a given percentage tip evenly, how
much does every one have to pay?
#+end_quote
#+name: tax calculation
#+begin_src python :results output :exports both :session *Python* :python python3 :tangle ../src/share.py
  raw = input("Enter bill, tax, party size:\n")
  parts = raw.split(sep=',') # using str.split()
  bill, tax, party = (float(p) for p in parts) # using a generator
  total = bill + (bill * tax/100) # using arithmetic operators
  share = total/party
  print(f"The share of ${bill} for {int(party)} with {tax}% tip is ${share:.2f}")
#+end_src

* Why learn programming?

- A valid question especially in times of generative AI! (A much
  discussed question among experts, see [[https://spectrum.ieee.org/learning-coding-age-of-ai][this IEEE article]].)

- What do you think?
  #+begin_quote
  1) Independence (self-sufficiency, freedom)
  2) Understand what AI does and control it
  3) Make tools to make your work easier
  4) Learn how to use machines for problem-solving
  #+end_quote

- What do I think?
  #+begin_quote
  1) Programming builds empirical, iterative thinking: it's
     immediately falsifiable ("Did it work?")[fn:1]
     
  2) Programming is a form of autonomy (freedom): you can turn your
     ideas into a working product without asking for permission,
     money, or someone else's tool.

  3) Programming is a form of agency (power): it is satisfying in
     itself to build something that works from nothing.
     
  4) Programming trains technological citizenship: hiring, credit,
     health, etc. are governed by IT and AI. Be the master of the
     machines, and not their slave.

  5) You can't verify what you can't read: AI makes mistakes and
     cannot reason about your specific problem.

  6) AI does not lower the bar, it raises it: getting something to run
     is easy now, knowing when and how to deploy agents is difficult.
  #+end_quote
  
- When and how did I start programming?
  #+begin_quote
  In 1979, when I was 16, my dad bought me a TI-99/4 home computer. My
  best friend had an Apple II Plus[fn:2]. We began to write computer
  games and eventually both ended up getting our PhDs in physics. It's
  fun to compare what Linus Torvalds, the creator Linux (and still its
  head honcho) [[https://www.brynmawr.edu/inside/academic-information/departments-programs/computer-science/beauty-programming][has to say about "The beauty of programming"]].
  #+end_quote

- What if you're not interested in Computer Science or computers as
  such?
  #+begin_quote
  The further you get into your field of interest - be it archeology,
  sociology, mathematics, exercise science or nursing - the more you
  will depend on computing resources and computational thinking,
  simply because we ask more and more from our machines. And they

* Why programming in Python?

- Popularity (see [[https://www.tiobe.com/tiobe-index/][TIOBE]] and [[https://spectrum.ieee.org/top-programming-languages-2025][IEEE]] indices of top programming
  languages).
  #+begin_quote
  1) Python has ranked #1 (or close) on the TIOBE and IEEE Spectrum
     indices for years - the language most likely to still matter by
     the time you graduate.
  2) A larger user base means a larger ecosystem: more libraries,
     more tutorials, more people online who can help you get
     unstuck.
  3) It's the default language of data science and AI/ML - the field
     this course is a stepping stone toward.
  #+end_quote

- Simplicity
  #+begin_quote
  1) Python's syntax reads close to plain English: no semicolons, no
     type declarations, minimal boilerplate standing between you and
     your first working program.
  2) That means class time goes to computational thinking, not
     fighting the compiler - the actual skill this course is trying
     to teach.
  3) It was explicitly designed for readability, which matters most
     when it's you, three weeks from now, reading your own code back.
  #+end_quote
  
- Expressivity
  #+begin_quote
  1) Python lets you express complex ideas in very few lines - one
     list comprehension can replace a five-line loop; your six-line
     tax calculator would take much longer to write in many other
     languages.
  2) Dynamic typing and a batteries-included standard library mean
     less code spent declaring types or importing basic
     functionality - more of your effort goes toward the problem
     itself, not the paperwork.
  3) Being able to prototype an idea in minutes and immediately see
     the result is what makes predict-run-modify learning possible -
     you experiment instead of just reading about syntax.
  #+end_quote

- Some things also speak against Python as a first language:
  #+begin_quote
  1) Dynamic typing catches fewer bugs at the point you make them - a
     type error Java or C would flag immediately might not surface
     in Python until the program actually runs that line, sometimes
     weeks later.
  2) It hides what's happening underneath: no manual memory
     management, no compilation step, so you don't build the
     intuition for how a computer actually executes a program - an
     intuition that matters once you reach a low-level language like
     C, or a systems/computer-architecture course.
  3) Indentation-defined blocks are elegant on the page but a real
     source of invisible bugs: mixing tabs and spaces, or
     copy-pasting code at the wrong indent level, can break a
     program with no visible cue.
  4) It's slow compared to compiled languages, which is invisible in
     a classroom exercise but becomes a real constraint the moment
     performance actually matters.
  #+end_quote

* Glossary

alone - too much to learn by heart. The only way to develop and keep
your tech vocabulary is to use the tech - in this case: by coding.

* Footnotes

[fn:1]Falsifiable vs. verifiable: 1) A claim is falsifiable if there
is one possible observation that would prove it wrong: "All swans are
white" is proven wrong if you can find one black swan. 2) A claim is
verifiable if there is some possible observation that would prove it
right: "There exists a black swan" is verifiable (find one), but not
falsifiable (you can never finish checking that none exist
anywhere). "All swans are white" is falsifiable but not verifiable:
you cannot test an infinite number of swans.

[fn:2]These home computers cost around $1,000 in 1979, which is about
$5,000 in today's money. To enter computing then was not a poor kid's
game. Today, you can start with a $5 micro:bit computer and
MicroPython, if you want the hardware, or just use your browser.
